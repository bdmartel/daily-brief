Today's lesson is two speeds in one poem. You were out leveling the lawn for seed, the wind loud and cool, and you felt two things at once: a race and a calm. A poem can hold both, and it does it with the lines themselves, not with the words race and calm.

The fast half uses three tools. First, run the sentence over the line ends, so the ear can't stop to breathe. Second, stack the verbs: hits, throws, drags, pulls, runs. Third, chain with and. The hill and the hedge and me. Every and is a gust that doesn't let up.

Then one short line by itself. I lean the rake against the tree. That is the hinge. The body stops, so the poem stops.

The calm half flips every tool. Short sentences, each one ending with a period. Long vowels you can't say fast: lies, long, cold, waits. Fewer verbs, and quiet ones. And the last line takes the wind's speed back and hands it to the air, so the poem ends still.

To try it yourself: write one thing that's moving, in a single sentence that won't stop. Then write one thing that's still, in sentences of five words or fewer. Put one plain action between them.
