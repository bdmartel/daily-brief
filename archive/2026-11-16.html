Good morning
Cold pine in the shop, that resin smell that gets into the wool of your sleeves and stays, sawdust gone sweet in the unheated dark where the stove hasn't caught yet. You stand there breathing it, the way a man does before he's spoken to anyone, the hills outside holding their gray a while longer. There's a small thing finished and set where you'll see it—a window cut true into the face of the board, three seasons deep. You turn it once in the flat early gray, thumbing the edge of what your own two hands made. The grain took your mark and held it— one of one, and the day's not even started.

Your day

Sunday, the fifteenth of November. There's something settled about a day like this, the kind that doesn't ask much of you up front and leaves the shape of it mostly in your hands. The season's done most of its turning by now, and what's left is quieter work, the sort that keeps rather than rushes.

Nothing's calling out as urgent today, so it's more a matter of where your attention wants to land. The kale, the skill saw, the regular saw — those are still sitting there whenever you circle back to them.

On the garden side, there's the slower fall rhythm humming along underneath everything — straw to pick up from Robbins, the gate still waiting to be measured and hung, and that border where the stones need pulling off so it can finally get mowed. Over at Tori's it's the same kind of patient tending: bittersweet to keep knocking back along the hill and the upper field, beds to feed with compost and a leaf mat before the cold really sets in, and the bare-roots that want potting up to overwinter. Further out there's the nursery order that opens early December and the grow-light setup to think toward for winter sowing.

Around the house and in between, the smaller threads are still loose — the plants upstairs, the mirror and frames in your room, the garage pile, a luthier to track down, sheets to make. None of it loud. Let the day find its own grain.

In touch

You had a nice back-and-forth with Sara about a thrift or market trip — you told her you'd grabbed her a couple of scarves and scored a big bag stuffed with blankets and scarves for ten dollars, and said next time she's coming along, though you were too maxed out for a second run with a blanket to serge down and a big cleaning day ahead. You reconnected with Austin about that board he gave you ages ago, letting him know you finally turned it into a flower press and checking what kind of wood it was. You also sorted out your smart plug troubles and let Bob know you're back up and running with routines on both ends. And you traded a bunch of messages with Kelly about coats and winter fabric, a green coat you're after, and some hunter-spotting along the way.

At the mirror

Here's a question worth sitting with: how often does the urge to leave a moment show up not when things are terrible, but when they're just flat — the slow email, the tense conversation, the Tuesday that won't end? That's the real test, Ben. Not the dramatic hardship, but the quiet pull to check out, to reach for the phone, to be anywhere but here. The thing is, your whole life is happening inside those unremarkable minutes, and every time you escape them, you're voting against your own presence. Staying isn't about enduring misery — it's about trusting that you're strong enough to feel the full texture of your own day, dull edges and sharp ones alike. You don't have to fix the hard or entertain the boring; you just have to stay in the room with them.

So look at yourself and say it: "I stay present with the hard and the boring instead of escaping them."
